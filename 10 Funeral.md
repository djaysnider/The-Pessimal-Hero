Marta O’Grady’s funeral was a quiet, dignified, wholly unexceptional affair, much like Marta herself.  While his mother had, thankfully, made the final arrangements for herself years ago, a tribute to her efficiency and self-reliance, Hal could only assume he was currently experiencing “Generic Funeral B” from a funeral home catalog.  She'd obviously picked her favorite colors for the flowers from a list of options and paid for a slight upgrade on the hearse -- she always did want to ride in a Cadillac.  He and his sister had very few family members left these days, which led to a sparseness of mourners, and his mother had been something of an anti-social recluse her entire life.  Actually, that wasn’t fair, Hal decided. She’d just been busy raising children, whether it was her own, other people’s, or just random stray animals she found on the street. When she couldn't find strays, she bought houseplants.  When she ran out of room for houseplants, she bought a pet rock.  It didn’t matter who or what she was taking care of. She was born to be a mother.  Still, if Hal were to guess, everyone here was a coworker or a friend of his sisters. Marta, herself, had only seemed to draw Uncle Benito.
Hal hadn’t attended many funerals, but he was fairly certain this was not how they were supposed to go. For months, he'd pictured a solemn, cinematic funeral---always rainy, full of mourners in impeccably tailored black suits and elegant dresses, hats tilted dramatically, enormous umbrellas shielding mascara-free tears. There might even be a mysterious stranger whispering cryptic words to the deceased before disappearing enigmatically into the mist. This funeral, however, had Bermuda shorts, black socks with white tennis shoes, and a baffling lack of tears. Everyone was perched uncomfortably on folding chairs, hypnotized by the slideshow of grainy photos featuring Hal and his sister as toddlers---stark naked, splashing gleefully in a kiddie pool. Rather than a dignified goodbye to his mother, this felt suspiciously like Hal’s personal and secret hell.
Hal hadn’t seen Father Alastair Fallon since he was a teenager, back when Fallon was one of those young, charismatic priests the diocese parachuted into crumbling inner-city parishes to inject new life or at least stop the bleeding of membership. At most, Hal figured there were maybe ten years separating them, which made him feel a little guilty, considering he was probably exactly the type of “lost sheep” Fallon had been sent to lure back into the fold. Even as a kid, Hal had liked Father Fallon, who was always armed with the perfect comforting words and Bible verses for any situation, making him easily the wisest person Hal knew and completely defying everything Alexandre Dumas had taught him about the clergy. Still, even Fallon's saintly charm couldn't keep Hal tethered to the Church. He'd managed to dodge every opportunity for confession since he was sixteen, ever since he first stumbled into a clumsy but thrilling encounter with Stephanie Burton in the rectory basement after a CYO meeting---a milestone he'd privately dubbed “the miracle of forbidden flesh.”
“... and Jesus said, ‘This is my commandment, that ye love one another, as I have loved you.  Greater love hath no man than this, that a man lay down his life for his friends. Ye are my friends, if ye do whatsoever I command you...” Fallon said with practiced poise and charisma.
As always, it was a good choice of scripture by Fallon, Hal decided. His mother had indeed been all of that.  She’d never even really had a life of her own.  It had always been as a mother, a caregiver to her own ailing parents, working for some overbearing boss, or whatever.  Aside from a few all-night bingo benders with Uncle Benito, she hadn’t even taken a vacation in the past ten years.
“The highest form of love is that noble self-sacrifice,” the priest continued. “To die for your fellow man, even a stranger. Not because your government told you to.  Not for the glory of a war or to win a battle.  No, he meant dying alone, unmourned, and unnoticed ... to help or save one person.”
Hal mentally checked out for the rest of the ceremony, vaguely imagining these same people returning here in a year or so to endure the same awkward slideshow. Lucy would surely insist on it. Even if Hal somehow managed to hide his death, she would hunt him down in the afterlife, demanding closure. He idly wondered if Father Fallon had any good scripture handy about girls’ volleyball coaches and debilitating neurological diseases. Hal resolved at once that he would have a higher quality of spectacle when his time came, even if he had to pay Barry to posthumously sabotage Lucy’s plans on his behalf.  First, no pictures of naked toddlers. Hal would select the pictures himself and possibly even make a video or two of thoughts, stories, and maybe a few limericks.  Better music, naturally.  No, wait, bagpipes. Lots of bagpipes. Finally, he needed something to make it memorable. It was, after all, his last word to everyone who knew him.  He decided would write a will filled with cryptic and utterly absurd instructions: “To my best friend Barry, I hereby bequeath the air hockey table---but only if he publicly admits that it was he, not Salieri, who murdered Mozart, and sincerely apologizes to a penguin for the crime.”  
When it was over, everyone gathered around him and his sister.  Each exchanged well-wishes and hugs, along with the ubiquitous promise of “Anything you need, just ask,” which Hal knew was something people said but no one ever really meant. They were “Hallmark Hopes,” a list of universally recognized unbinding promises that no one can be legally held to, along with “Let’s get together soon,” “I'll think about it,” or “I love you.”  He could vouch for his friends, though he was detached enough from his sister’s life that he could not identify any of the people who claimed to be hers. It was just as likely that half the people in the room were crisis actors kept on standby by the funeral home to pad out low-attendance funerals and make the customer feel better about the expense and who were paid in free food and booze at the wake.
“Geraldo, it’s good to see you, though obviously not the best of circumstances.”
Hal looked up and found himself face-to-face with Father Fallon.  “Hello, Father. I’m sorry. It has been a while, hasn’t it?”
The priest smiled sincerely, “Yes, and Benito says you’ve been having a crisis of faith lately. I will pray for you to get through it and find peace.”
Uncle Benito doesn’t know half of it, Hal thought. He still hadn’t summoned up the courage to tell anyone in his family anything about his condition and impending death. He had planned to say something once the shock of the funeral died down, as he knew his mother would chastise him for stepping on her big moment.  “Well, I will try to drop by for mass tomorrow,” he blurted out, then ran through his memory to make sure that particular promise was on the non-binding list as well.  “A lot is going on right now. I could do with a bit of your wisdom.”  The thought of going to confession terrified him as much now as it had 20 years prior. What was THAT session going to look like?  Would he spend the rest of his life doing Rosaries and Hail Marys instead of working on the bucket list he was beginning to form in his mind?   “Come to think of it, I haven’t even seen the new sanctuary.”
“That ‘new sanctuary’ is fifteen years old.” Fallon chuckled. “But we’re always happy to welcome souls back home. Particularly when our flock seems to get smaller and smaller every year.  I know, it doesn’t help that the neighborhood is a bit run down now, with most people moving out of the city and into the suburbs.  And, of course, despite my best efforts to ply them with offers of complimentary recharge stations and a once-a-month chassis wash and polish, I can’t seem to get the scooter gangs any closer than the parking lot.  They’ve tormented quite a few of our members into staying home from night services altogether.”
Hal noted two scooters leaning against a tree a couple of rows over.  No, he thought, those were probably legitimate rentals, not the ones the gangs had high-jacked and jailbroken.  “I’m sorry to hear that. If there’s anything I can---” He cut himself off, realizing he was just contributing to a flurry of Hallmark Hopes at this point.
The priest touched his shoulder and turned to leave. “It’s kind of you, son.  Sometimes, I feel like this city is becoming infested with hate. The police are powerless to stop all of it, and good people don’t seem to care anymore. We’re taught in seminary to be shepherds of a flock and tenders of a garden. There’s only so much you can do when everyone wants to be a weed, though.”  He stared in the distance with profound sadness, caught himself, and forced a smile. “Sorry, there’s enough sadness here today. We can talk more at the wake.” He smiled sadly, then added, “You will be breaking into your mother’s legendary stash of Akvavit, won’t you?”[^fn1]
Hal merely nodded, unable to tell if there was more to the priest’s words than intended.  Funeral conversations never seemed to be real.  He, himself, had already falsely confessed to five girls, and one boy about having a crush on then in the fifth grade, simply because it seemed a polite way to make small talk with someone he hadn't seen in decades.  He watched the priest leave, avoided making eye contact with Lucy as she hugged and cried into the shoulders of everyone around her, and almost jumped out of his skin when Barry’s giant hand landed on his shoulder.  “You doin’ okay, buddy?”
“It’s a lot, man.  I suppose I’m doing as well as possible with everything going on right now.”
“I haven’t told anyone. You know ... about your issues. Just in case you’re worried.  I gather from their reactions that you haven’t even told your family yet?”
“How can I?  They’re still trying to get through all of this. They feel guilty enough because they’re secretly relieved it’s finally all over for Mom.  They don’t need me to start all that up again in the middle of this.”  Hal thought for a minute.  “In fact, I don’t think I’m going to say anything until it manifests and is noticeable, and maybe not even then.”
“Well, that’s your call.  I’ve got your back no matter what, but that’s your family.  That’s what family is there for.  You can always change your mind.”
“I know, and thanks.”  Hal accepted a bear hug, even though he hated hugging, and the two started down the hill toward the car.

[^fn1]:  Yes, the main character has a Latino first name, an Irish last name, and a family who obsesses over Swedish liquor.
