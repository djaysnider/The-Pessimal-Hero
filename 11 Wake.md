“Isn’t funeral food the best?” Benito exclaimed, shoving his face with his third plate of cocktail sausages. “I’m glad Lucy inherited your mother’s recipe.  That woman always did know how to lay out a spread for a party.”
Hal nodded noncommittally and scanned the room.  There seemed to be about a dozen more people at the wake than attended the funeral.  As before, he didn't recognize half of them. He caught sight of Lucy on the other side of the room, who shrugged as if she knew exactly what he was thinking.  The line at the liquor cabinet was especially popular.
“Uncle Benito, do we know who any of these people are?”
“Oh, most of them are from our Bingo posse.  Next to the end-of-month chili cookoff nights, wakes are our second most popular get-together.  By the way, I brought a couple of bottles to replenish your mother’s stock, but you’re going to be out of Akvavit by the end of the day.  Trust me.”
Removing Akvavit from his life would be the one bright spot of all this, Hal thought as he scooped some potato salad onto his plate. He never liked the smell or the taste of the stuff. It was as if someone poured liquid hate into a fancy bottle and slapped a label with a picture of a fjord on it.
“So, have you decided what you’re going to do with your inheritance?”
“Inheritance?” Hal half-laughed. “Well, Lucy and I figured we’d get together next week and pick out what was worth keeping. You should probably come over and claim whatever you want to keep before then.  After that, we figured we’d donate everything else to the consignment shops and sell the house to one of those spec buyers for 30 or 40 thousand in quick cash.  Why?”
Benito’s jaw dropped.  “No, I meant ... wait ... you really have no idea, do you?”
Hal shook his head, “Idea about what?’
“She told me she was never going to tell you two, but I just assumed you would have read the will by now.”
“No, we were going to go over all that next week. Why?”
“You know about your grandfather, Pierre, right?”
“Some kind of chef, wasn’t he?”
“Some kind of chef, you say.  Was Picasso just some kind of painter?  Was Tito Puente just some kind of drummer?  Was Don Pendleton just some kind of writer?  No.  Your grandfather invented a way to make runoff industrial concrete into an edible confectionary spread.”
Hal winced at the thought.  “Who would eat something like that?”
“Who, indeed? Well, maybe you have little faith in such an invention, but a certain Kellogg’s Corporation saw the potential and paid him a kingly sum for this secret.  What do you think they’ve been spreading on top of Pop-Tarts for the past 60 years, eh?”
“You’re kidding me.” Hal started to laugh.
Benito's expression was deadly serious. “Nope. Last I checked, after expenses, taxes, and splitting it up, you and your sister stand to inherit about 750 thousand dollars each.”
Hal’s jaw dropped as his heart leaped into his throat. Well, that was a thing. Even if he invested it in low-yield bonds, the annual interest would yield more than his current salary as a P.E. teacher -- not that there would be an “annual” anything for him ever again. “I had no idea. She certainly never lived like she had that kind of money.”
“Well, she was a practical woman, but where do you think the money for both your college educations came from?”
“Wow, it all makes sense now,” Hal said dumbfounded.
Bennito leaned in conspiratorially. “Listen, my boy, take my advice and don't just squirrel away your inheritance in your sock drawer like your mother did, hoarding every penny as if St. Peter was going to ask for exact change at the gates. You’re barreling headlong into middle age with no wife, no kids, and frankly, even your houseplants look a bit skeptical about your long-term prospects. This is your chance! Go wild. Buy yourself a young Russian mail-order bride. Hell, buy two or three! Go full Mormon and assemble your own personal United Nations delegation of marital bliss. Imagine the multicultural breakfasts alone: chocolate chip blini on Mondays, sashimi pankeki Tuesdays, gefelte blintzes on Wednesdays... And why stop there? You could eat them on your yacht painted like a volleyball with a really annoying name like ‘Sets on the Beach,’ ‘Ace of Waves,’ or 'Teacher’s Pension Blues.' Now, if you’ll excuse me, I've spotted Cousin Margaret making an aggressive beeline for the Akvavit. That woman drinks like prohibition's about to start again. If I don't intercept her, the entire Swedish spirits industry could collapse. Just remember, great wealth should come with great irresponsibility. Leave ‘em with a laugh. Priorities, Hal, priorities! Good talk!”  With that, he vanished into the crowd, leaving Hal stunned, blinking, and wondering if Lucy had received an equally baffling and vaguely inappropriate motivational speech. 
Barry appeared behind him, grabbing an empty plate from the stack and eyeing the buffet.  “You still doin’ okay?”
“I dunno,” Hal muttered, staring at the back of Uncle Benito’s head.  “Apparently, I’m rich now.”
“Really?” Barry said, unimpressed.  “How rich?”
“Well, if you add my life savings, which I was about to cash in and put towards my bucket list, I would be about 760 thousand dollars rich.”
“Hey, you could buy that Karch Kiraly rookie card you always wanted.”
Uncle Benito returned, handing Barry and Hal each a small glass of a pale, yellow liqueur that left vapor trails behind it as it moved.
“What's this?” Barry asked, sniffing the glass gingerly.
“Akvavit,” Hal said, “And, hopefully, it's the last of it.  He said, putting the glass down and trying to hide it under a plate of nachos.
“What's in it?”
“Does it matter?  It’s Swedish. It was invented one winter by a bunch of people who got snowed into a log cabin for six months with nothing but a still and a bag of root vegetables and spices.”
Barry nodded. “So, it's like Vodka.”
“No, Vodka is fine.  Vodka does whatever vodka says it's going to do.  That stuff is evil.”
Barry took a tentative sip and winced. “Oh my god!”
“I tried to warn you!”
“It tastes like someone dipped a rye cracker in chai tea and lit it on fire!”
“It's foul, I know. It's like being mugged by a reuben.”
“Why does your mother even have this in her home?”  He took another sip and grimaced less than last time.  It was obviously growing on him.
“It's a long story and involves Vikings.”
“Sounds cool,” Barry said as he finished his drink and retrieved Hal's from under the plate of nachos.  “So, how's your bucket list going?”
Hal shrugged. “Not bad.  So far just the usual stuff: Play in the Bass Circle on the Weaver Bay boardwalk.”
“Nice one.”
“Disney World, but stay at the Star Wars resort instead of the Motel 5.”
“Excellent.”
“Oh, and I’ve always wanted to try the Triple Bacon Arrhythmiaburger challenge at Gary’s Grease Shack.”
“Dude, that’s horrible for you, it’ll --” Barry cut himself off.  “Oh, sorry, man.”
Hal waved it off.
“I guess now I could buy my own Gary’s Grease Shack franchise or even hire Gary to be my personal chef.”
“Yeah,” Barry said, wincing in disappointment, “you definitely need to be thinking bigger.”
“So, what would you do if you were me with all this money?”
“Oh, I don’t know. Buy a boat and sail the Mediterranean. Play baccarat at a casino on the French Riviera. See if Casablanca is anything like that movie.  Go dancing at a rave in Ibiza.  Oh, you always loved Greek Mythology. What about going to Athens and seeing all the cool stuff you only read about in those boring-assed books you love?”
“Yeah, I suppose,” Hal said, unconvinced.
“Why wouldn’t you?”
“I don’t know.  It would be fun, but, thinking about it, I don’t want to do any of this stuff by myself.  Seems kind of ... sad and lonely.”
Barry held up a finger.  “I tell you what. I’ve got some vacation money saved up and plenty of time off I can take.  You make the list, and whatever it is, we’ll do it together.”  He thought about it for a moment.  “Up to $3000 in my case, though.”
“You might regret that, you know,” Hal said.
“Nope.  You have my solemn word, my friend. Whatever you want to do, I’m your sidekick, no matter what.”
Hal laughed for a minute, wondering what he could find that he would enjoy and that would simultaneously irritate Barry to no end. It was funny; he’d only found out about his inheritance a few minutes ago, but now it seemed like this was going to be the distraction he needed to keep him from spending the rest of his life worrying about how many days he had left and how long before he had to face his inevitable end.
Honestly, even the thought of seeing Greece, the majesty of Athens, Crete, and even Delphi, didn’t seem worth it now.  What good is the experience if you won’t enjoy it or remember it for much longer?  No matter what he did, he was still headed for the same fate that befell his mother.  A descent into madness and, in the end, a pointless death to cap off a pointless life.
He found himself wishing Vicky was there, but how do you ask someone you’ve only known for a few minutes to come to your mother’s funeral?  Still, she seemed to have a better grasp on the situation than anyone else he could be talking to, and the only time he’d felt alive and worthwhile in the past several years was when he stupidly and almost inadvertently stood up to those scooter-riding thugs.  It would have served him right if they had shot him dead right then and there.  At least it would have spared him the next year watching himself decline.  It might have even scared one of them straight and done some good in the world.
Hal froze.
Suddenly, he knew exactly how he wanted to spend the rest of his life.
