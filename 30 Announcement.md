“What the hell is going on?” Vicky heard Hal say over the comm.
“No idea, Punkin’, but he’s somehow hijacked Channel 9’s signal.”
The voice on the hijacked feed droned like a caffeinated doomsday preacher, “... suffering and indignation of the residents of the Riverside neighborhoods. While their promise of a sports complex may one day bring prosperity, the near future sees even more pain and suffering at the expense of the wealthy elite as local businesses are forced to sell their livelihoods in the name of progress or suffer and die as construction makes it all but impossible for them to do business.”
On the screen, Vicky saw Hal trying desperately to get Smythe’s attention.  From the side camera view, she saw Smythe roll over like a disgruntled walrus, clearly uninterested in playing secret agent any more this evening.  She checked the police feed.  “Punkin’, you got three squad cars arriving in about ninety seconds, you need to get gone.”
“Understood,” Hal replied, then she heard him make a vague, non-binding promise to share with Smythe anything they found out, punctuating it with, “Ummmm ... I'll call you,” as he climbed back into the Roadster and sped off.  “Okay,” Hal said, turning to the onboard cameras again, “who is this guy and how the hell is he doing that?”  
“I’m no expert,” Vicky said, “I’m no broadcast engineer, but even digital TV can get hacked if someone gets close enough with strong enough gear. Think of it as a digital plumbing disaster. No matter how fancy the pipes, one bad Mexican dinner can still ruin your bathroom tile.”
“I thought TV signals were digital and encrypted now,” Barry said.
Vicky shrugged. “Yeah, but those encryption keys are about as hard to get as your grandmother’s Wi-Fi password. Any good hacker would have three taped to their fridge door for a rainy day.”
 “So why doesn’t this happen every week, then?”
Vicky thought about it for a moment.  “Because most hackers aren’t close enough to the transmitter to overpower the studio’s uplink with a stronger signal.”
“How close do they have to be?” Hal asked.  Vicky saw that he had already turned around and was heading downtown.
“The studio’s on top of Empact tower,” Vicky said, pulling up a map of the area.  “They’d need to be within a couple of blocks and have a direct line of sight.”
“Well, that narrows it down to a couple of square miles and about thirty buildings,” Barry protested.
“Maybe not,” Vicky said, studying the Hierophant’s transmission, which was still going on with some vague nonsensical muttering about the nature of man in the post-industrial revolution.  “Those curtains.  How tall would you say they are?”
Barry regarded them for a moment.  “Can’t see the top, but at least twelve feet if we use him as scale.”
“Okay, so we need someplace that has blue velvet drapes and at least twelve-foot ceilings.  That room’s too big to be an apartment or hotel room, too private to be a lobby or public office, so we can knock out about half of the buildings in the area.”
“I’ve got it,” Barry cried.  “Hotel Beauchamp on 3rd Avenue.  It’s been abandoned for years, but back in the 1940s boasted the Cobalt Ballroom up on the top floor, which became a seedy punk bar called Cerulean Dreams in the 1970s and finally Club Indigo in the 90s.  It closed down about five years ago and has been on the market ever since.  Now, guess what the main decorative feature of all three venues was.”
“Blue velvet drapes?” Vicky asked.
“Blue velvet drapes!” Barry said, fist pumping the air in victory.
Vicky pursed her lips.  “Now, how did you happen to know all that, Muffin?”
“Power of Google, baby!  You gave me one job, and I did it!”
“Awww, see? You’re not just pretty.  Remind me to give you another job soon,” Vicky said condescendingly and turned to the mic.  “Punkin’, head for the Hotel Beauchamp.  He’s in the ballroom on the top floor.  I’m sending it to your GPS now.”
“Should we tell Smythe?” Barry asked.
Hal shook his head. “Not yet.”
Barry winced. “I suppose I should ask the obvious questions of why we’re charging headlong into this, and why we’re doing it without backup.”
“Because I have a feeling this one’s our fault,” Hal said, his voice dropping.
“Our fault?” Vicky asked?
“He calls himself the Hierophant. That’s a Greek priest, the same way Eidolon is a Greek spirit.  He’s calling me out by using a name like that. I think he’s a copycat.”
“Holy crap, Hal, you’ve got yourself an arch nemesis! A Joker to your Batman!  A Lex Luthor to your Superman! A Doctor Domino for your Wonder Woman fetish!”
“Or we’ve seriously messed with somebody’s head and pushed them over the edge.”
“All the more reason to call for backup,” Vicky said. “Get some expert head-shrinkers in here before we do even more damage.”
Hal shook his head, “Let’s see what we’re dealing with first. It could be nothing.  Maybe he’s just a well-read AV geek out for a thrill.”
“Or maybe he’s a serial killer,” Barry offered.
