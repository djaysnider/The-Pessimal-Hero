The intersection of Adams Street and 25th Avenue was no place to be after dark and definitely not a place to be after 11 PM.  It was well after midnight when Helen exited the 24-hour laundromat.  She hated being out of the house that late at night, but some unruly customer had flung an entire plate of spaghetti on her waitressing uniform. Worse, she could verify that it wasn’t in the least bit cold, as he had complained, so the entire tirade had been pointless, just a show of machismo on his part.  Unfortunately, as it was the third time that week this had happened, though not always spaghetti, it had left her with no clean uniforms for her breakfast shift in the morning.  Inevitably, this meant she was out in the worst part of town, on the worst night of the week, at the worst time of night, just to get laundry done in the few hours she had between shifts.
The laundromat was located in a strip mall between Burning Wheels, a seedy biker bar -- open until 4 AM -- and what she considered to be a particularly stabby taco truck that served food until the bikers left and went home. Despite being a haven for customers practically around the clock, the landlords still hadn’t bothered to repair any of the nine towering streetlights that, in proper working order, would have illuminated the parking lot in something equivalent to artificial daylight.  Instead, after a dozen or so bulbs, at approximately $30 a piece, were shot out by bikers, the people with the checkbooks surrendered patron safety to fiscal responsibility.
She settled her purse into the crook of her left arm and managed to fold and pile all three loads of laundry into one basket, which she balanced between her wrist and bicep.  This left her right hand free to unlock the car and open the door.  These were, of course, minor feats to someone who regularly carried six hot plates of food on one forearm and balanced a tray of drinks over her head in her free hand.  Pity there were no more marketable uses for these skills other than waitressing.  She paused for several minutes inside the laundromat, waiting for a group of four bikers engaged in a shoving match to depart before she risked walking to her car.  Unfortunately, while Friday nights were ideal for getting all the machines to yourself, it also meant she was forced to park at the far end of the packed lot.
She was only halfway to the car when the first wolf whistles came, not from Burning Wheels, but to her surprise, from behind the taco stand.  The inevitable cries of ‘Hey, baby!” and “What have we here?” followed.  She pretended not to notice and made a beeline for her car.  Sadly, while she was perfectly capable of amazing feats of balance with impossibly awkward objects, much of this required the sacrifice of speed and agility.  The four punks had split up, two blocking her way forward, and the other two her exit.  She was pinned between two cars.
“Don’t worry, lady.  We just want the purse,” the largest of the four said, brandishing a long hunting knife.  She found that somewhat insulting.  First off, she was only 24, and while he didn’t call her the dreaded “ma’am,” certainly “lady” implied the same deference to an elder, which she was certain she was not.  Second, while a tattered t-shirt and uncoordinated faded sweats bearing her old high school mascot weren’t exactly the type of thing one wore to attract the attention of the opposite sex, she knew for a fact that a sizable portion of the male biker and taco-eating community thought the sweats hugged her hips just perfectly and made her ass look great.  Okay, she didn’t wear a bra on laundry nights, and she wasn’t about to drop everything she was carrying and ask her assailants for an honest assessment of her physique, but she was pretty sure, even in her current state, she was at least a strong seven, possibly a wholesome-looking eight.  There was no reason for this leather-clad attention vacuum on wheels to fall back on calling her “lady.”
Helen slowly and carefully put down the laundry basket. It would be a shame if something spilled and she had to start the entire process over.  Satisfied the clothes were secure, she slit the purse down her left arm and held it out for the human exhaust pipe to take.
“Excuse me,” a gruff voice said from behind her.  “I seem to be lost.  Could you direct me---” 
Everyone turned to see the newcomer and began laughing instantly.  None of them believed their eyes.  There, stepping out of the shadows about two car lengths away, was a gray mass of a figure wearing a hooded trench coat, leg paddings, and combat boots.  He extended two thickly gloved hands in sort of a shrugging gesture to indicate he was unarmed.  The voice was a bad affectation, like a child making fun of an adult by talking like them.  Clearly, this person was completely unstable.  It was mid-July, 90 degrees, and somewhere around 120% humidity.
“---to the path of righteousness!” he continued, though nobody heard him over the laughter and pounding on the car in hysterics.  
The man continued to walk towards them, completely unfazed by seeing four guys, obviously bigger and tougher than him, all carrying knives.  Clearly a mental case, Helen thought.  Probably one of the homeless people living in the alley behind the drug store who had hit the hooch a little bit too hard earlier.  Still, he was the best-dressed homeless person she’d ever seen.  The boots alone probably cost $100, and the coat looked like a custom job.
“What the hell is up with this guy?” one of the punks asked the “leader.”
The one closest to the newcomer suddenly stopped laughing.  “I don’t know,” he said, voice shaking, “but with a coat like that in this heat, he could be packing a rifle, maybe even an AK under all that. I don’t want anything to do with that.  Let’s go!” He snatched the purse from her hand, and all four ran in the opposite direction, across the street.
“Aw, come on. Sonofa--” the hooded figure muttered in what appeared to be his natural, much more girly, voice as he started after them.  
What kind of a nutbar runs after armed muggers? Helen thought to herself as she collected her laundry, thankful that the would-be assailants only got away with a dollar-store purse and a few sales receipts.  She unlocked the car, threw the laundry basket in the back, and walked around to the other side to get in. Her key was almost in the ignition when she saw someone waving their arms frantically at her from across the street. 
“Hang on!  Wait!” a voice cried.
The mysterious stranger, the hooded gray figure, was limping back towards her, holding her purse in his hand.
“I thought you might want this,” he said, this time using a different, affected growl in between panting from exhaustion.  She took the purse from him and threw it in the passenger seat.  “I’m sorry they got away with your wallet,” he added, “it’s just really uncomfortable running in these boots.”
She reached into her right sock and pulled out a small wad of bills wrapped around her driver’s license and held together with a money clip.  “The purse was empty.  I only carry it as a decoy in case I get mugged.  I’ve got a half-dozen of them at home.  They cost me $4 each.”
“Will you shut up,” the gray man moaned.
Helen shot him a look. “I beg your pardon?”
“Oh, no, sorry, I wasn’t talking to you,” the gray man said, still trying to catch his breath.  She still couldn’t see much of his face under the hood.
“Are you okay?” 
“Yeah, just ... a lot of ... I wasn’t expecting ... they were very fast,” he panted, then turning to his right where no one was standing shouted, “I said shut up!”
Okay, she thought, crazy man talking to voices in his head.  Best to humor him and get the hell out of here. “Well, it was very brave of you to come to my rescue like that, Mr. ...”
“Eye-de-lawn,” the man said, or at least something that sounded like that.
“Idol-on?”
“I-DOLE-un!”  He pulled something out of his ear and yelled at it, “NOT ONE MORE WORD!”  Okay, probably a bluetooth earbud, she decided.  Maybe not crazy then, at least.  Still, politeness dictated he should hang up the phone when talking to someone else.
“Your throat sounds terrible. You should get some Robitussin at the drug store on your way home, Mr. Egg Dole---”
“No, E-I-D-O-L-O-N!”
“Ah, Mr. Eidolon!  Sorry, it’s really hard to understand you with that laryngitis thing.”
“No, no Mr.  Just ‘Eidolon.’”
“Is that your first name or your last name then?”
“It’s my ... it’s a nickname.  Eidolon.  It’s like a ghost in Greek mythology.”
“A ghost.”
“Yes.”
“Oh, okay.  My name’s Helen Spiros, it’s also Greek,” she said by way of small talk. “How did you get that nickname?”
“I just sort of came up with it.”
“You can’t just give yourself a nickname.” She grimaced, pulling back slightly.  “That’s just sad.”
“It’s not exactly a ... oh, never mind.  Have a good night.”  The strange man turned and walked back into the darkness the way he came.
