The GPS said, “Are you sure?” in a quivering voice as Hal parked the roadster in a hotel parking lot at the top of a hill on Seigel Boulevard.  Between the stock anti-theft system and Vicky monitoring the car’s surroundings via cam, he was pretty certain it was safe hands, but there was nothing wrong with an extra bit of caution in a neighborhood that looked more like a discount Hunger Games arena.  
The near northwest side of the city was distinctly marked by the presence of a good-sized river that once stood as a major thoroughfare of barge traffic from the industrial center of the city to the seaports down south.  When rail and air travel became more common, and the steel industry gradually moved overseas, it became the neighborhood that mugged its own watch, a barren wasteland of factories, decaying homes, and unmaintained streets.  It would have been an irredeemable hotbed of criminal activity if the population was any denser.  As it was, most of the soil was so contaminated with industrial runoff that the buildings and surrounding grounds had been condemned and marked as unlivable years ago.
Still, there were businesses that survived, mostly to serve the small population that remained, for one reason or another.  The primary focus of the neighborhood was warehousing and parts depots, with a spattering of grocery stores, gas stations, and garages for good measure.  Hal decided the local park, still a reasonably popular hangout with under-aged kids, would be his best shot. It was conveniently located in the middle of a reasonably nice residential area, well ‘nice’ for a free-range crime district, and would thus see a number of potential marks for the aspiring vagabond looking to break into a life of evil doing.
He holed up behind a gardener's shed in the corner of the park grounds and listened to the “pop symphony” of what were either late-July fireworks or gunshots.  It offered a view of the entire playground area where a group of four kids, probably not even teenagers yet, were playing basketball under a streetlight.  The four streets approaching it were all eerily dark, with only the sparse light from the occasional window providing any light at all.  Barry had spotted it in the satellite view for Google maps and suggested it. Hal felt sure Google maps did not to this neighborhood justice.
Hal sat, Indian style, against the building. A dumpster and shadows blocked the players’ view of him while still allowing him to spy out from the narrow gap between the dumpster and the side of the building.  “They don’t seem too concerned for their safety,” Hal offered.  “Maybe this isn’t the place to be for dispensing justice.”
“I dunno, I have a good feeling about it,” Vicky said via the comm-link in his ear as she watched the back of his knee via nipplecam.  “The streets are nice and dark, and there are a limited number of lit areas in the park.  I say sit tight.”
“It’s boring, it's creepy, and I skipped dinner,” Hal complained.  Even though it was a bit of a disaster, his previous outing had been much more action-packed.
“Oh, stop whining,” Barry said in his ear.  “Did you ever hear Batman whining about a lack of action when he was on patrol?”
“Batman had Robin. I’m stuck with you two: Captain Swaggerpants and the Sass Ninja.”
“Hey, you’re getting’ better at the comebacks, Punkin’,” Vicky said supportively.
“There’s a Caramel Munch Bar in the pocket over your right kidney. Did Robin ever do that for Batman?”
Hal tore the wrapper off and was attempting to chew his way through the inch-thick bar of caramel and chocolate when he heard the unmistakable high-pitched whine of trouble coming towards him from across the street to his right.  “Sfuder nang,” he said emphatically.  “Ngo, flez, met it ne u sfuder nang.”
“What?” asked Vicky.
“Nufng,” Hal said as he got to his feet and crouched further into the shadows. Sure enough, not two minutes later, four leather jacket-wearing thugs riding motorized personal scooters sailed across the street, over the curb, and into the park itself.  They made a beeline right for the kids playing basketball, stopping under the lamp post at the edge of the court.  There were taunts and shouts, but Hal could not make out exactly what was being said.
“Looks like there’s shrubbery on the other side of the shed. You should be able to follow it all the way down to the court without them seeing you,” Barry offered, though Hal had already spotted the hedge and was halfway there.  He stayed hunched over and took a position at the edge of the court opposite the gang and their helpless victims.  The scene had begun to deteriorate into pushing and shoving, the younger kids attempting to go home, but the scooter gang was blocking every avenue away.  He wasn’t sure what they wanted. The kids wouldn’t have any money or anything of value.  Perhaps they were just bullies looking to make a name for themselves.  Bullies were inherently cowards, so this should be no trouble to break up, and then he could then go on with more pressing matters.
He pulled the gaiter up over his nose and mouth, made sure his hood was correctly angled for maximum effect, and casually strode out from behind the bushes into the court.
“Mrfrz me. Ib dem shom nineuf brflm eer?”  He stamped his foot in anger, he was now 0 for 2 at dramatic entrances.
He heard screams of laughter from Barry and Vicky in his ear.
Everyone on the court jumped and turned to look at him, no doubt mistaking him for a madman.  He made an effort to swallow the last of the Caramel Munch Bar and work its sticky remnants from his teeth before speaking again.
“What the heck are you supposed to be?” One of the thugs asked, stepping away from the group to approach him.  It would have been the perfect opening for the victims to run and escape the situation, but frankly, they seemed equally curious.
“The Eidolon,” Hal said in his clearest, deepest, most eerie voice.
“Ooooh, Punkin’, nice job with the new voice,” Vicky swooned in his year. “You’ve been practicing that haven’t you?”
“What’s Aid-alone?!” the kid asked.
“Come here and find out,” Hal said, maintaining his eerie voice.
“Now you sound like a child molester, Punkin’.  Maybe stop talking.”
Hal was fine with that, however, there still wasn’t exactly any action to speak of.  The victims and the bullies still stood in place, staring at him, occasionally making a gesture as if they were going to pounce, which, while it may have been more effective if they were actually closer to him, didn’t do much in the way of intimidation from 20 feet away.
Hal was beginning to get frustrated. He couldn’t very well just attack a bunch of teenage kids, but, like the muggers the previous night, nobody really seemed keen on attacking him, and he was beginning to take offense at it.
“You guys gonna make a move or what? You’re supposed to be tough,” he said at last.
“What are you, some kinda cop?”
“Seriously, do I look like a cop?”
“Well, no. What are you supposed to be anyway?”
“I’m the Eidolon.”
“Yeah, yeah, you said that like we’re supposed to know what that is.”
Hal sighed. “You guys never read books, do you?”
“No, I mean is that some fancy new word for rent-a-cop or somethin’?”
“No, I’m a masked vigilante!” Hal protested.
“You mean like Batman?”
“Yeah, like Batman.”
“So ... you have superpowers then?”
“Batman didn’t have superpowers.”
“Yeah, but Spider-Man did.”
“I would classify Spider-Man as a superhero, not a vigilante, though.”
“So, you don’t think Batman was a superhero?” the kid asked?
“Most of his stories, aside from the ones where he was in the Justice League, would not necessarily put him in the superhero realm, no.  The stories are written with a realism to them that, in my opinion, do not fit the milieu of the superhero genre.”
“Spider-Man had realism.”
“I mean yeah, Spider-Man had the whole teenage angst thing with rent, acne, and Aunt May dying of the same heart attack for forty years, but he also had the whole clone saga and that ‘Spider-Cide’ fiasco, so not really.”
This, at least, caused the gangbanger to stop and consider his position. 
“Are you gonna fight these guys or argue comic books all night?” A frustrated Vicky sounded in his ears.
“What about Daredevil?” one of the young basketball players offered.  “He had superpowers but was rooted more in reality.  Would you consider him a vigilante?”
“No, he had superpowers, so he’s a superhero.  Punisher, on the other hand, is a vigilante.”
Everyone seemed to agree with the ground rules that had been laid out.
“So, you’re not a cop, and you don’t have superpowers then,” the thug asked, circling back to review.
“Nobody has superpowers, that’s all fiction.”
“Well, if you don’t have superpowers, why are you trying to start stuff?”
“I’m protecting innocent citizens from punks like you,” Hal said.  At last, they were getting back to the point.
“But you’re not a cop?” the thug asked for the third time.
“No, I’m the---”
“I-doll-un.  Yeah, you said that.”  He conferred with his fellow gang members for a few minutes, and then they all started walking towards him.  Hal felt his muscles stiffen, poised to strike as soon as called upon. Strangely, the four victims, now completely left alone, were still not running away. Instead, they watched the scene with anticipation. “Forget it freak. If you’re not a cop, just give us your wallet,” he said as all four pulled out knives. Two carried medium-sized buck knives; the other two had butterfly knives and made it a point to show off the same flourishes and tricks that everyone masters about 30 minutes after picking up a butterfly knife for the first time.
For some reason, Hal instinctively reached for his back pocket to hand them his wallet, then thought better of it and stopped himself.  “I’m afraid I don’t carry a wallet.”
“He’s not carrying a wallet!?” One of the kids in the back moaned.  “Let’s just go back to the shed.  It ain’t worth it tonight.”
At this point, Hal was at his wit's end.  Last night’s prey had simply run away; tonight’s didn’t even seem to be interested.  “I might have a wallet,” he said.  “What if I lied?”
“Oh, Punkin’,” came the voice in his ear. “You really that eager to take on a group of what are probably high school kids?”
The apparent leader was somewhere between confused and impatient now. “Okay, if you’ve got a wallet, why won’t you just give it to us?”
“If he doesn’t have a wallet, why would he pretend he did, though?” the one in back countered.
“Shut up, Joey.”
Hal pressed them on.  “Either way, whether I have a wallet or not, you’re not getting it, so you might as well go back to your little honeycomb hideout.”
“No,” the leader said, coming to his senses.  “Now I HAVE to know if you have a wallet, and I have to kick your ass because you’re being a jerk about the whole thing.”
Finally, Hal thought to himself and slid his right foot back to put him in a solid but not obvious defensive position.  He didn’t even raise his arms until the last minute when the “leader” lunged at him with the knife.  Then, sliding to one side, he grabbed the attacker’s wrist, punched the inside of his elbow to bend the arm, and directed the knife deep into the fleshy part of his attacker’s thigh.  The thug screamed, probably more in surprise than pain, and loosened his grip on the knife just long enough for Hal to retrieve it from his leg before he hit the pavement.
To his surprise, his movements felt second nature to him, and his attacker wasn’t nearly as adept as Barry was during their training.
To the other three, it appeared that their leader simply tripped and fell. Hal realized, from their vantage point, they probably hadn’t seen him retrieve the knife, which he now held, reverse-grip in his right hand.  Joey, as he had heard the leader call him, held back while the other two charged, knives extended in front of them.  Hal sent a side kick into the closest one, knocking him off his feet, and slashed out at the forearm of the other one, scoring blood with a not-too-serious but painful cut about two inches below the wrist.  The punk maintained his grip on the knife, pivoted, and came back to face Hal, stepping just to his left to keep the other two out of Hal’s vision.
Hal didn’t play the game, however.  Shifting the knife back to a forward grip, he turned and gave his fallen attacker a swift kick to the groin to ensure he’d stay down, in the process creating what appeared to be an opening for his knife-wielding opponent, which the inexperienced thug fell for.  Hal was ready.  Against his better instinct, he brought his free arm up to deflect the blade with the armor plating in his arm, then slashed down with his knife hand, scoring another hit.  His attacker’s hands would soon be covered in blood.
The one called Joey had apparently come to his senses and ran off back the way they had come. The leader, nursing a bleeding leg, was crawling towards a bench to help him get to his feet.  The other was still writhing in the gutter, clutching his groin.  Time to offer the truce, Hal thought, knowing you can’t be lucky in a knife fight forever, and his element of surprise was gone.  “It’s just you and me.” He waved the knife randomly in front of himself to discourage his last opponent from advancing.  “That’s an awful lot of blood, and your knife’s gonna get slippery in a minute.  You might want to make a strategic retreat at this point.”
“I don’t think so,” his opponent said.
“You’re being foolish.”
“No, I’m stalling you.” He grinned as the sound of scooters echoed from the street Joey had retreated down.
Hal shook his head and mentally scolded himself.  It was a lot of scooters, at least eight, he figured.
“Exit time, Punkin’,” Vicky said over the earpiece.
Hal deftly folded the butterfly knife back up in three moves and chucked the sheathed handle at the leader’s head, where it struck with a resounding crack and yelp of pain from the thug.  Then, he darted into the shadows of the cross-street.
There was no initial sign of pursuit as the only one left standing had gone to retrieve his scooter.  Within a minute, however, he’d seen the entirety of the gang round the corner approximately half a block behind him.  They were closing fast.
“Okay,” Barry said over the earpiece, “we know you can’t run far or fast in the armor, and you’ll never take on eight or nine at once without the element of surprise.  You’re going to have to evade.”
“No kidding,” Hal huffed back at him darting down a driveway between two houses.
“Nuh uh, Punkin’, satellite shows that yard is fenced in.  In fact, MOST of the yards in this neighborhood are fenced. Stay in front of the houses and head for the next street, then turn right.  I got you.”
Barry chimed in. “Stay in yards and off the sidewalk though, it’ll keep them at a distance and give you the trees and shadows for cover.  You might be able to lose them.”
Hal did as he was told and left the sidewalk for the grass-covered lawn, where he instantly caught his foot under a garden hose, tore a sprinkler out of the ground, and half-rolled, half-tumbled into a flower bed.
“Okay, sure footing then. Maybe use the sidewalks when things aren’t lit up?”
Hal swore under his breath, got to his feet, and kept running.  He heard the scooters and the shouts of the goons chasing him.  They couldn’t be more than twenty yards behind and getting closer every second.
Finally, he reached the intersection at the next street and, as Vicky had instructed, turned right.    Cutting diagonally across the corner lot while the scooters stuck to the road bought him a few more seconds, but already he felt his lungs begin to burn with the effort.
“Fifty more yards and a sharp left, Punkin’,” Vicky instructed.
He was running alongside the river’s floodwall, which was the only thing on his left.  What was she expecting him to do?
“See it yet?” She asked calmly.
There it was: a staircase leading to the top of the floodwall.  Well, the gang may follow him, but at least the scooters wouldn’t.
He was halfway up the stairs when his pursuers arrived.  Ditching their scooters on the curb, they started up after him.
“Uh, guys,” Hal protested between what were now desperate gasps for air, “They’re still behind me.”
“Not to worry, Punkin’.” He could almost hear Vicky’s grin. “I said I got you.”
Hal reached the top of the stairs and found himself face to face with two headlights speeding down the service road towards him.  “Oh crap, who the hell is that?!” He said, dismayed.
“That’s me, Punkin’,” Vicky said as the car slammed on its brakes and skidded to a stop. “Get over there!”  Finally, he recognized the familiar black shape of the Avanti Roadster. The driver-side door was swinging open for him.
“What the hell?” he asked, still winded from the run.
“Told you I got a buddy to send me the specs.  I’ve got complete control of the remote starter, the GPS, and the self-driving system.  With the cameras, I can pretty much drive the thing from here.”
“Amazing!” Hal was laughing as he jumped into the driver’s seat and shut the door just as his pursuers reached the top of the stairs.
He threw the car into reverse and backed up the service drive, then turned down an access ramp to street level below.
“Where you goin’ now?” Barry’s confused face was on the monitor next to him.
Hal smirked. “Just one quick community service to perform,” he said as he gunned the engine, turned a hard right, and barreled down the street towards the bottom of the stairs, taking special care to smash through the row of nine scooters that were parked there.
