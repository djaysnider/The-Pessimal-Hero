Hal pulled the Roadster into the alley behind Hotel Beauchamp and parked next to a loading dock. The building was rife with 1930s art deco columns and arches that just screamed “haunted.” “Do we have a floor plan of this place?” he asked.
“Working on it, Punkin’.”  There was a long pause while she pulled up the schematics and sent them to the in-car display.  “Aside from the front entrance, there’s a taxi and valet stand on the East side of the building, and a fire exit from the maintenance room on the west side.  No wonder this place has been deserted for years.  It’s a fire marshal’s worst nightmare.”
Hal checked the loading dock doors.  They were all locked.  He jumped down and made his way around the west side of the building.  Sure enough, one of the doors to the maintenance area was ajar, propped open by an old mop bucket.
“Well, here’s how he got in, classic supervillain mistake, I suppose,” Hal said and slipped through the door into complete darkness.  “Where am I going from here?”
 “Door on your left, take the hallway all the way out to the main lobby.”
Hal felt around in the dark until he found the doorknob and stepped through. The emergency lighting that gave him a general view of where the walls were without betraying the shadows that were, for now, his friends.  He followed the hallway down to the grandiose main lobby, still appearing much as it did in the 1940s. “Wasn’t this place closed down because it was supposed to be haunted or something?” He just wanted confirmation now, in case a man wearing one side of a masquerade mask suddenly appeared singing a rousing chorus of “All I Ask of You.”  It would be embarrassing were he to try to punch out a ghost, mistaking it for his arch-nemesis.
“It’s not haunted,” Barry said, reassuringly.  “It was zombies.”
“Oh,” Hal sighed.  “Swell.”
“You’ll be fine,” Vicky said.  “The elevators are on the north end of the lobby.”
Hal made his way across a dusty floor to the elevators and pressed the call button multiple times.  There was no sound to indicate any machinery had been activated.  “No power to the elevators.”
“Stairwell on your right,” Vicky said.  “The ball room is on the eighteenth floor. Remember stealth. He’ll be able to hear you coming.”
“I really hate stairs,” Hal moaned.
“Good for your glutes, Punkin’.”
Hal opened the door to the stairwell and peered inside.  It was completely dark aside from a faint light far above, which he assumed to be the eighteenth floor. A murmur of voices echoing down the tall, stone shaft constantly reminded him to move slowly and keep to the shadows to avoid detection. He slowly ascended for what seemed like half an hour, whispering each floor number as he passed for the benefit of his cohorts back in the command center.
Finally, upon reaching the top floor, he stopped next to another propped-open fire-door and peered through to a grand ballroom with a cathedral ceiling. Giant windows afforded a picturesque view of the city, which twinkled happily, as if not knowing what evil was about to befall it.  Blue velvet drapes framed each of the windows, except at one corner, where they had been drawn.  There he saw a half-dozen figures, all dressed in similar theater masks, hastily packing up AV equipment and lighting rigs like the disgruntled stage hands of an off-Broadway play that got cancelled on the second night.
“Oooh wait,” Barry said. “Have you thought through your big entrance?” 
“My big entrance?” Hal whispered annoyed.
“Yeah, this is the first meeting with your arch-nemesis.  You need to storm in there all hero-like and demand he stop his evil ways or face your mighty fist of justice or some such thing, right?”
“I don’t think so,” Hal protested. “At the rate my big introductory speeches have been going, they're just going to mow me down with machine guns about three words in, don't you think?”
“Yeah, but this is important. Plus, you're due!“
“Well, when you’re out here in the field, then you can make whatever grand entrance you like.  I’m going for a stealthier approach.”
“Suit yourself.”
Hal slid through the door and hugged the wall.  There was very little natural cover in the room, so Hal moved very slowly in the shadows to avoid attracting attention.  Distracted with their packing duties, the henchmen rarely stopped to examine their surroundings.  The one Hal recognized as the Hierophant stood in front of one of the grand windows, motionless, staring out over the city.  Hal was easily outnumbered.  His best hope was that the henchmen would start carrying equipment out and leave him alone to apprehend, or at least talk to, the Hierophant.  He cursed himself for not thinking through what he was going to do if there were too many people for him to take on at one time.
“I wondered if you would be able to find me, Eidolon,” The Hierophant said without turning around.  “I’m impressed.  Nice detective work.”
“Thanks. Nice drapes,” Hal said. “I’m behind, though. What’s exactly the evil plan here?”
“Didn’t you listen to my presentation?  I thought I made my intentions clear.”
“Well, to be honest, I really hate distracted driving.  I mean, I got bits of it?  Something about destroying the city, getting the people to rise up against the oppressive elite, that kind of thing.”
“So, tell me, are you like the eidolon of Aneas in armor doing battle in his stead until he can be healed and return?  Or are you the eidolon of Patroclus the deceased wanting to be buried so he can cross the gates of Hades?”
Hal thought about it for a moment.  “To be honest, neither.  Actually, maybe a bit of both?  Seems to me you've put more thought into my name than I ever did.  I'm flattered.”
“I'm disappointed,” The Hierophant said.  Hal imagined the man actually frowning behind the expressionless mask.  “I had hoped you’d be my intellectual equal.”
“Oh, I wouldn’t write me off so quickly.  I may yet surprise you.”  Hal noted that the henchman having finished packing up the equipment, had left the ballroom without comment, leaving the two of them completely alone.
“Well, once the city is ours, we will have plenty of time to discuss a marketing and PR angle, I’m sure.”
“Yeah, about that,” Hal said matter-of-factly, “I’m not so sure about this proletariat uprising thing of yours.  That never seems to work out very well for anyone involved.”
“Oh, believe me.  I am a student of history, my friend. I’ve thoroughly studied the oppression of the working class at the expense of the wealthy elite. I am a student of the Pullman Strike, the Bread and Roses Strike of 1912, the Air Traffic Controller Walkout, and the Major League Baseball Strikes of ‘94 and ‘95.  History will be on the side of the oppressed laborers!”
Hal rubbed his forehead in confusion. He needed to stall for time while he figured out what to do. “Yeah, well, maybe you should have consulted with me before coming up with this scheme of yours then.  Any chance I can get you to stand down so we can talk and plan it out better?” 
“Stand down? It’s too late for that, everything is already in motion.  The plan is perfect.  There’s nothing else to consider.  You have to trust me.”
“Oh, I trust you.  Trust you to do something stupid like send all your henchmen away right before a fight.”
“Fight?!  I’m a religious symbol, not a warrior!  You’re the Eidolon, avatar of the warrior, sent by the gods to aid man in their greatest battles!”
“The only battle here is you and me, Hierophant. I’m trying to save innocent lives here, not to get them killed.”
“There are no innocents!” the Hierophant shouted. “There is only those with power, and those who take power.  Those who sit out the war and cowardly let others fight their battles for them do not deserve a place in the world to come.  Action is a moral imperative. I would have thought you of all people understood this. Seriously, it's right there in your name!” The Hierophant shook his fists in frustration.  “This isn’t right!  I spent weeks planning this.  It was you who gave me the final piece: my identity -- our shared motif, if you will.  We’re joined in this.  How can you stand there and oppose me like that?!  I thought you saw the genius of our great mission.  You stood up against oppression and the police.  You understood that we must take our destiny into our own hands.”
“I really think you misunderstood me,” Hal said in his most calming voice.
“Apparently so, but no longer!” the Hierophant yelled, holding his hands up as if in incantation.  “I see you now for what you are, foul spirit.  Not an image of a hero sent by the gods to do battle on behalf of the fallen, but the idol of Helen herself, who eggs others on to war, which you watch from your tower on high as legions die in reverence to your name.  Fine, so be it.  If you will not live by the sword, you will die by the sword.  My legions will consume this city, and you along with it.”  With that, he pulled a device from behind his back and cast threw it towards Hal.
There was a loud “WHUMP” sound and a flash of intense light.  Hal instinctively fell to his knees, blinded, deafened, and incredibly dizzy.  When he finally came to his senses several minutes later, he could barely make out the sound of Vicky and Barry yelling at him over the comms.  The earpiece probably saved his left ear from the worst of the effects of the flashbang.
“Okay, okay, I’m up.  Give me a second,” Hal said shaking the cobwebs from his mind, which only seemed to make him dizzier.
“Thank heavens,” Barry said.  “How bad did it hit you?  Can you function?”
“Little dizzy and I’m seeing spots.  My right ear is still ringing.  I think I can drive though, if I can find the door to get out of here.  Any idea where our friend went?”
“They left in a van and headed south.  I followed them via traffic cams but lost them just outside the loop,” Vicky said.
“How do you want to play it,” Barry asked.
“Let’s call it for the night and hit it hard first thing in the morning.  It’s been a long day, and I have a feeling our lives just got a lot more complicated,” Hal said turning the Roadster towards home.
