There was a high-pitched whining in his ears.  There was a throbbing pain in his head.  He tried to open his eyes, but the light stabbed into his skull like it was armed and deeply offended by his existence. The pain shot through him, each nerve firing off a strongly worded protest about his recent life decisions.
Hal O’Grady, formerly the Eidolon and currently the world’s most reluctant paperweight, tried to assess his situation. He was in a hospital bed. That much was clear. Both arms and one leg were in casts and suspended like grim holiday decorations. Whatever drugs they’d given him had done a pretty good job of making him not care about the pain.  His limbs ached incessantly, but honestly, he wasn’t sure if they were still technically attached. They floated there, waiting, like backup parts on layaway.
Breathing was quite intensely painful.  He could tell there was some kind of wrapping pressing down on his chest.  Broken ribs, no doubt.  Probably a punctured lung, which would explain the ventilator going down his throat and the tube in his nose that was no doubt feeding him strained carrots or some kind of food he hated at this very moment.
His mouth felt weird, and not just because there was a giant ventilator tube stuck in it.  A quick probe with his tongue confirmed he was missing most of his teeth, probably owing to the fact that his right eye and cheek were completely caved in.
Oh yeah, the baseball bat.  How could he have forgotten about that?  It seemed to be a high point of the evening.  Silly to forget about it so quickly.  So that was it, eh?  His grand plan for a quick, painless, and anonymous death in service to an innocent human being was down the tubes. He’d missed it by one angry Asian chef.
“Oh, you’re awake,” a familiar voice said from a part of the room where Hal couldn’t turn his head to look.  “Part of me really hoped you wouldn’t come out of it, but a part of me really wanted to talk to you.”  The voice became clearer, and Father Fallon, whom he had not seen since his mother’s funeral, walked into view.  How nice it was for him to come down.  He really wished he’d followed through on that promise to come to mass last month.
“You’ve been in a coma for almost a week,” Fallon said.  “I brought you here after the riots.  You see, when you did what you did, practically gave up your life to save me...” he trailed off.  “Me, the guy who caused so much trouble and got all those people injured and killed.”
Something’s not right, Hal thought.  The drugs were really good, but he’d never seen Father Fallon at the riots, and the only person he really saved was the Hierophant, and even THAT had been a mistake.  His eyes widened when the drugs finally allowed him to piece what should have been a completely obvious equation together in his head.
Seeing Hal’s eyes widen in fear, Father Fallon held up a hand in assurance, “I didn’t tell them who you were, don’t worry.  After you fell, I ran away.  I was so humbled ... ashamed, I guess ... at what you had selflessly done for me.”  He caught himself, shook his head, and tried to start over.  “I’m sorry, I should start at the beginning.  My name is Father Alastair Fallon.  I’m the priest at St. Leonardo in Riverside.”
Fallon didn’t recognize him, Hal realized.  Apparently, his injuries were so horrible and disfiguring that his own priest didn't recognize him.  For that matter, since the Eidolon carried no ID, couldn’t speak with the ventilator, move his arms to write, and had no remaining dental work to serve as identification, would anybody ever be able to identify him?
“As you probably guessed,” Fallon continued, “I never meant you or any of the citizens in Riverside any harm.  I just couldn’t stand all the crime, and the blind eye the police were turning to pretend things weren’t bad and getting worse.  Worst of all, that damned sports stadium, the great golden calf, coming into my neighborhood, buying out my church...” he took a deep breath, trying to collect himself.  “I saw you on the news that night, and you really did inspire me. I had most of a plan put together, but the people were the one factor I couldn’t be completely sure about.  I guess you could say I had trouble putting my faith in them.”  He laughed slightly at the irony.
“I had to make them care.  Care about the library.  Care about the church.  Care about standing up against crime and vandalism in their own neighborhood.  More than that, I had to make them act and do something about it on their own.  That was the part I wasn’t sure about, at least until I saw you that night, taking on the scooter gangs.  I’d already been working with them for some time.  I persuaded them to unite.  Stop fighting among themselves and become a singular force.  When you caught that the Maccabees fought the priests of the Greek gods, you were right, but the gangs were never intended to be my Maccabees, and frankly, I doubt they noticed the irony.  No, the people of Riverside were my Maccabees.  The scooter gangs were just sheep. They were the stone, and I was the sling, if you will.  Thanks to your example, I was certain that if I drove the sheep to the slaughter, the people really would rise up and decide they’d had enough.  In one night, I could do what the police had been unwilling to do for the past three years: wipe all of those disgusting gang bangers out once and for all.”
“We are building the new temple of Jerusalem there in Riverside, and it’s all thanks to you.  Now that they’ve seen the power they wield, they’re standing up and appreciating their community again.  They came out in force to help rebuild the library, and thanks to my timely arrival in the middle of the riots to bring peace and order, they’re finally coming to mass again.  Of course, you were busy bleeding out at the time, but oh, you should have seen me, Eidolon.  I gave a magnificent speech that calmed the crowds, the cops arrested the remaining scooter gang members, and then everyone joined together to put out the fires and start the cleaning up.”
“The best part is, now that everyone has a vested interest in their community again, next week, they’re bringing a resolution to the council to withdraw the bid for the new stadium.  They want to build their own temple, not have someone else come and build a false temple in Riverside.”
“It’s all because of you, though, Eidolon.” He removed a vial from his satchel.  “I’m sorry to say, the doctors aren’t optimistic about your chances.  Mr. Han broke just about every bone in your body, and your internal injuries are even worse.  Heck, for all we know, you’ve got a traumatic brain injury and haven’t understood a word I’ve said.” Fallon smiled to himself.  “That would be funny.  I'm going on and confessing all my sins, and they aren’t even understood.  I just wanted you to know that you did make a difference, and your sacrifice did not go unnoticed.”  He hefted the vial and chanted softly in Latin.  “I don’t know your faith, but because you seemed to know the story of Maccabees, I assume you went to Sunday school at some point, so I thought the least I could do would be to come give you a blessing for all you did and all you meant to me, at least as much as I can without revealing my role in it.  I know it’s not the pomp and circumstance you probably wanted and that your actions deserve, but, as they say, that’s life.”      -- Extraneous Stuff		3
