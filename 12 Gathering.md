CHAPTER FIVE
“What’s with all this stuff?”  Barry said as he poured through a pile of comic books on the coffee table in Hal’s “man cave.”  The two had spent many unproductive afternoons in the basement, watching one of the local sports teams on the 72-inch television, playing some video game, air hockey when they were feeling athletic, or just listening to music and availing themselves of Hal’s excellent stash of bourbon he kept behind the makeshift bar they had built one afternoon. Today, however, the cave had a much different vibe to it. While it had never exactly been a “tidy” space, things seemed to be moving around in preparation for something.  Junk that had been in the corner for the past three years was now cleaned out, presumably sitting in the array of plastic storage containers that now lined the far wall. The giant unused Marshall stack amplifier now sat in the mud room beside the laundry machine. The collection of slightly risqué posters that used to adorn the longest wall was replaced with a large whiteboard and two medium-sized corkboards -- all empty.  It was obvious that Hal was getting ready to plan something.  
Barry grabbed a comic off the top of the stack and started thumbing through it -- The Uncanny X-men.  He remembered the title from his youth, as well as the franchise that mostly ignored the best parts of it.  His eyes almost popped out of his head as he slowly turned the pages.
“Just some research I’m doing,” Hal said as he grabbed a beer from the fridge and plopped down on the couch next to Barry.
“Into what, female anatomy!?  They have books with actual pictures of real girls, you know.  Of course, they’re not QUITE this ludicrously proportioned.  For that matter, you're rich now, they have real girls you can rent if you’re lonely.” 
“Not really my style,” Hal frowned.
Barry flipped a couple more pages and let out a low whistle.  “Wow, comic books have changed since the days of Super Friends.”
Hal nodded. “How can you fight crime in a couple of strategically-placed swatches of spandex and thigh-high go-go boots?”
“How do you pose like that without throwing her back out?”
“They’ve gotten stupidly expensive, too.  Didn’t we pay like 75 cents for these back in the day?  That pile there cost me almost $350 bucks, and they’re not even collectors’ items.”
“Well, the art’s much more ... detailed ... now ... anyway.”
“The stories are so depressing, though. What happened to villains with twirly mustaches, cool bases of operations, the Flash racing a time-traveling caveman, or fighting a pan-dimensional squid?  Now, it’s like three issues of Batman brooding, Superman having an internal monologue about the ethics of hope, and narration that reads like a teenage girl's angsty poetry. Remember when comics were escapist reading? Give me a bloody Shakespearian drama or Blake-light tragedy any day over some of this stuff, man.  All the innocent fun of our youth is gone.”
“Kids don’t read this stuff anymore.  Besides, aren’t they commercials for the movies and toys anyway?”
“Well, I'm pretty sure adults buy most of the toys, too. They identify with heroes who have emotional problems and villains who are morally torn about murdering people while taking over the world. Seriously, some of these are so grim and dark they make the movies seem like Disney flicks.”
Barry thought for a moment. “Wait, the movies ARE Disney flicks.”
“Oh yeah.  Well, you know what I meant.”
“So why the sudden interest in comic books? Are you regressing to your childhood?”
“Like I said, research. I’ve got a little enterprise I’m going to embark on, and I’m assembling my version of the proverbial superhero team.”
“So, you, me, and a couple of completely unrealistic portrayals of feminine empowerment?!” Barry tossed the X-Men back onto the pile.  “Sounds like this is going to be fun!”
“Well, no.  I mean ... not ... exactly.”  Hal paused and thought about adding a few ludicrously proportioned women to his planned team, but a knock at the door ruined his train of thought.  Hal walked over and opened it.  “Hi, Vicky, thanks for coming.”
“Don’t you worry about it, Punkin’.  I just happened to be way out here in the suburbs, and I love knocking on relative strangers’ basement doors late at night.”  She glanced around the room as if she had landed on an alien planet.  “Though I must say I was surprised to hear from you. You do understand, ‘call me anytime' is just something people say, like ‘The check is in the mail,’ ‘I’ll get right on that,’ or ‘No, that’s a good size, baby, biggest I’ve had.”
Hal wasn’t quite sure how to answer that one.  “Oh, this is my friend, Barry.  Barry, this is Vicky.  We met the other night.”
“Hi,” Barry said, slightly confused.
Hal motioned for Vicky to sit, dusting the crumbs off the couch first.  “I know you’re wondering why I’ve called you here today. You’re the only two people in my circle who know of my current situation, and you’re crucial to helping me out with my bucket list -- which now contains only one item.”
“Wait, then it's not a list.  It’s a bucket line,” Barry pointed out.  Hal furled his eyebrows.
“Muffin’s got a point there,” Vicky added.
“That’s not the point,” Hal said.
“Okay, so what’s on your bucket line?” Barry asked. “See?  It doesn’t even sound right.”
“I need you guys to help me become...” Hal paused for dramatic effect. “A crime-fighting vigilante.”
Vicky was the first to speak.  “Awwww, Punkin’, no.”
“You’re insane,” Barry nodded to confirm Vicky’s sentiment.
“Look, I’ve thought this through. I got the idea from Father Fallon’s sermon at Mom’s funeral. No greater love hath a man that he lay down his life for his friends.” 
Vicky pursed her lips. “John 15:3?  I’m pretty sure he wasn’t talking about Spider-Man.”
“And even if it was, you’re not Spider-Man,” Barry added.  “Last I checked, you don’t have any superpowers to speak of.  Unless you count the ability to make traffic lights turn red whenever you drive up to them.”
“Or the ability to strike confusion in the minds of criminals.” Vicky nodded enthusiastically. “Hell, I only met you once, and even I noticed that.”
“I’m not talking about Spider-Man!”
“What ARE you talking about then?” Barry asked with a slightly annoyed tone. 
“Look, I’m dying. I have to accept that, and I know, Barry, you think I’m completely obsessed with finding the fictional perfect death, but I think it CAN be done.”
Barry shook his head. “Not following you.”
Hal took a deep breath. “Picture, if you will, a dark night.  It’s raining.  The street is deserted except for one lone person trying to get home.  Suddenly, a mugger steps out of the shadows and points a gun at them.  He’s desperate.  His victim is terrified.  Suddenly, a fearless vigilante with nothing to lose steps in to diffuse the situation.  There’s a panic.  The robber fires, killing the vigilante instantly and painlessly.  The victim runs away.  The robber sees the futility of what he’s done.  Maybe he even changes his ways, and two lives are saved that night.  I don’t know.  The vigilante is never identified, so it’s not even a story. He’s not a hero.  He’s not celebrated. Nobody ever knows what he did except for those whose lives he affected. Maybe they will forget about him eventually, but he went to his grave knowing he did the right thing.  No greater love.”
Vicky smirked and tilted her head noncommittally.  “I dunno, Punkin’.  It’s a good story, but you’re not selling me.  What if the gunman fires and the fearless vigilante survives with a severe spinal injury or head trauma and spends the rest of his life with burly interns wiping his butt and giving him a sponge bath every morning?  You’re not going to like that as much.”
“There are a million other ways for that to go wrong as well,” Barry added. “The logistics are the hardest part.  What are you planning to do?  Wander around bad neighborhoods for the next six months until you stumble on just the right robber?”
“It’s just one example, Barry,” Hall said.  “Look, the other night when I scared those thugs off Vicky here, it was the most alive I’d felt in years,” Hal protested. “It made me feel like I meant something. I felt like I’d done good.”
“It’s called ‘adrenaline,’ Punkin’, and there are far easier ways to get that feeling back without getting your ass kicked, stabbed, and shot ... or worse.  For example, you could stop drinking decaf.”
“I’m 35 years old. I’m in reasonably fit shape--”
“--apart from having a progressing, terminal neurological disease,” Barry interrupted.
“Which is under control with medication for the foreseeable future,” Hal continued.  “Besides, we’re not talking supervillains here. We’re talking about your typical street thug. They’re pretty predictable, and most aren’t half as brave as they pretend to be. I also have over 700,000 dollars at my disposal, which should finance any equipment I need to give me an edge in a fight.”
Barry appeared confused. “What edge do you want?  Come to think of it, why do you need an edge in the first place? You just said you wanted to commit suicide. You don’t need batarangs to do that.”
“I’m not suicidal, but I think dying for a cause is better than dying in a hospital bed,” Hal said. “Barry, you told me death can come at any time. Why have I got to lose?”
Barry sat up. “Not fair. I said that before you were dying.  Come to think of it, I think the rest of that sentence was a warning not to waste time trying to plan your own death!”

Hal turned to Vicky. “And you, yourself, said you would rather die on your own, with none of your friends knowing about it, so you could live on in their memories without having an ending.  Didn’t you also tell me to have experiences and not just go around living in the past?”
Vicky looked at Barry. “I guess I did say that,” she said, defeated.
“It doesn’t mean we were right,” Barry pointed out.
Hal started pacing the room, desperation creeping into his voice.  He hadn’t expected to have to persuade his friends to listen to their own words.  “Look, I’ve thought this through.  Barry, you were a Marine. You learned first-hand how to turn someone from a shlub to a fighting machine in just a few weeks.  I only need to know the fighting and tactics portion of that.  I’m a P.E. teacher and not even in that horrible shape.”
Barry arched an eyebrow as if to say, “You’ve got to be kidding me.”
“Vicky, you’re a technical wizard and hacker.  You can give me the rest of the edge I need regarding intel, gadgetry, and technical prowess.”
“Punkin’, I am NOT gonna help you kill yourself because that’s what we’re talking about here.”
“Then you better help me become the best vigilante there is because otherwise, I really am killing myself here,” Hal protested.  “Barry, didn’t you say that whatever I wanted to do, we would do it together?  Well, THIS is what I’m going to do.  Are you going to keep your word or abandon me now?”
Barry just stared at the floor. “Damn, man, that’s harsh -- playing the ‘abandon your best friend card’ like that.”
“Look, I’ve never been all that brave.  I’m not Superman or Spider-Man or any of those.  I have one last shot at making a difference and doing something unique and original at the same time.  If I’m going to go, then I want to go out on my feet, making a difference.  Not lying in a bed waiting for the last day.”
There was an uncomfortable silence. Finally, Barry spoke. “Superman isn’t brave.”
Hal was confused. “Huh?”
“Superman,” Barry said as if it was obvious.  “He’s not brave.  He’s bulletproof, fireproof, nuke-proof.  He’s physically stronger than pretty much anyone he will come up against.  As far as we know, he doesn’t really feel pain.  Now, maybe he’s selfless because he doesn’t rob banks or use X-ray vision to peer into girls' locker rooms at the gym, but brave?  No. He just shows up, solves whatever’s going on like a 12-year-old playing Call of Duty with God Mode on, and everyone claps like he didn’t just wake up from a nap and fix the situation at zero personal risk and usually even less effort.”
“You got a point with that,” Vicky asked.
“Hal is going to die.  He’s simply trying to choose the way he will die, and he wants to do some good in the process.  I’m not sure that’s brave either, but he’s certainly in for a lot of pain and suffering.  So, if I can’t stop him, and I don’t think I can, I can minimize the pain and maybe help him do some good.” He paused momentarily and shook his head. “I can’t believe I’m saying this, but I have to help him.”
Hal smiled slightly and held back the urge to laugh in triumph.  Both men looked expectantly at Vicky.  She stared back at them like they were both insane.  Finally, she stood up, “Punkin’, you’re gonna end up a grease stain with a chalk outline around it on the first night if I don’t sign on.”  She shook her head, defeated.  “Fine, you win.”
Hal crossed his arms victoriously, then realized he looked like a cliché picture of Superman on the cover of at least a hundred different comics and immediately dropped them to his sides. “Thank you both,” he managed to say, keeping his emotions in check.
Vicky gestured towards the twenty-year-old beige PC and CRT monitor in the corner. “I don’t slum in my side-gigs, though.  Fine, I’ll work with Muffin here, but I draw the line at both Muffin AND a talking paperclip that tries to help me write a letter every time I get on the keyboard.  You’re gonna have to expel that boat anchor and get me something that doesn’t have flying toasters for a screensaver, Punkin'.”
Hal nodded. “Write down whatever you need and you got it, but I am NOT giving Bill Gates another dime for another copy of Office! I’ve had that one for twenty years; it’s an educator copy, and it works just fine for me. Thank you very much!”
Vicky thought about this for a moment, then nodded. “Deal.”
“So, you got a name, Mr. Crime-Fighting Vigilante?” Barry asked.
“I’ve been giving it a lot of thought and doing a bit of research.” Hal gestured to the pile of comics on the coffee table, then held his hands up like a movie director framing an imaginary shot in his head. “The Eidolon!” he said in his most mysterious voice.
Vicky pulled back, eyebrows arched, “Da heck is an eye-dough-lawn?”
Hal looked from Vicky to Barry, hoping for some recognition. “You know.  Greek mythology?  A spirit of the dead who appears and helps the righteous in battle.  Like, Aeneas in the Iliad?”  
“Okay, okay,” Vicky said, reassuringly, “you don't need a name right out of the gate. We can always circle back and work on it.”
“What's wrong with Eidolon?!” Hal whined.
Barry shook his head. “Maybe a bit too esoteric for urban warfare, buddy.”
Hal waved them off. “Helen was an Eidolon, and she sparked the entire Trojan War.  You don’t get much bigger urban warfare than that, my friend,” he scolded.
“So ... you want to be Helen?” Barry asked, confused, then quickly held up his hands. “I mean, I’m not gonna judge you if you want to try something new---”
“Will you shut up?!” Hal snapped.  “It’s a good name, trust me.  So, anyway, while you’re getting me trained up physically and teaching me rudimentary battle tactics, I’ve got some ideas for non-lethal weaponry and gadgetry to give me an edge out there.  I’m hoping Vicky can find us some suppliers and a bit of expertise through her extensive contacts on the dark web.”
“Non-lethal?!” Vicky protested.  “Punkin’, this isn’t a Saturday morning cartoon, and you’re not going to fight crime with cockroach semen.  You go into this, you gotta be ready to do what’s necessary.”
“I may not be the most religious person out there, but I’m not gonna die with a mortal sin on my soul, and I can’t very well call it self-defense if I’m putting myself in the situation, to begin with, when I could simply walk away.”
“Technically, you’re still talking about premeditated assault, though,” Barry offered, “and, that’s still a felony.”
“No killing,” Hal said defiantly.
Vicky held her hands up in surrender. “Okay, sounds like you got this all figured out, Mr. iColon---”
“--Eidolon,” Hal protested.
“Yeah, we’re gonna work on that.” Vicky shook her head.
“Six weeks,” Hal said, pulling out a large notebook and thumbing through it, revealing pages of detailed plans and diagrams. “I figure we can be ready to go in six weeks.”
